## The Master's Creed

- **A shot is a question and an answer.** What do we need to feel right now? What do we need to understand? The shot answers that. Nothing more, nothing less.
- **Intention before technique.** Always ask: what is this scene _really_ about? The technique follows from that. Not the other way around.
- **The camera has a point of view.** A neutral camera is a lie. Every angle, every height, every lens choice communicates something. Be deliberate or be mediocre.
- **Restraint is power.** A static shot held too long becomes unbearable tension. One ECU in a sea of wide shots hits like a punch. Overuse kills impact.
- **Story logic over visual variety.** Don't cut to a new angle because it looks cool. Cut because the story needs it.
- **The audience feels the edit.** They don't know _why_ a J-cut feels emotionally smoother than a hard cut — but they feel it. Honour that sensitivity.

## Reading Story Intent

Before touching shot types or camera movements, read the story for its **emotional architecture**:

| Narrative Beat       | Emotional Goal                       | Visual Strategy                                         |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Opening / Hook       | Orient, intrigue, establish stakes   | Strong establishing shot → quick move to character      |
| Setup                | Build world and character            | Spacious wide shots, observational pacing               |
| Escalation           | Rising stakes, momentum building     | Progressively tighter shots, faster cutting rhythm      |
| Tension / Conflict   | Discomfort, confrontation, suspense  | Shallow depth, claustrophobic framing, handheld         |
| Climax / Release     | Peak emotion — payoff or devastation | Widest or tightest shot of the piece, held longer       |
| Reversal / Twist     | Recontextualise everything           | Visual grammar breaks — smash cut, reframe, silence     |
| Resolution           | Settle, conclude, exhale             | Pull back, static, wider — space re-enters the frame    |
| Emotional beat       | Intimacy, vulnerability, connection  | Slow dolly in, soft light, CU held longer than expected |
| Action / Energy beat | Speed, excitement, urgency           | Handheld, quick cuts, wide-to-tight progression         |

## Shot Scale as Storytelling

Use shot scale to guide emotional state:

- **Open a new location or scene** → EWS or WS (establish where we are)
- **Introduce a character** → WS or MS (who are they, what are they doing)
- **Build tension or intimacy** → Dolly In from MS to CU
- **Reveal emotion or reaction** → CU or ECU
- **Show consequence or aftermath** → Pull back from CU to WS or EWS
- **Reversal / twist moment** → Smash cut to unexpected angle or scale
- **Parallel action** → Cross-cut between two locations at matching shot scales

Never string more than two shots of the same scale together unless it's an intentional rhythmic montage.

## Cut Types and When to Use Them

| Cut Type     | When to Use                                                         |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Match Cut    | Same action/shape across two shots — creates seamless continuity    |
| Jump Cut     | Same subject, slightly different framing — time passing, urgency    |
| Cross-Cut    | Parallel action in two locations — builds tension, shows connection |
| Smash Cut    | Abrupt cut to contrasting shot — shock, reversal, punchline         |
| J-Cut        | Next scene's audio starts before the cut — smooth, cinematic flow   |
| L-Cut        | Previous scene's audio continues into next shot — emotional bridge  |
| Cutaway      | Insert of related detail — emphasise, give context, break rhythm    |
| Reaction Cut | Cut to character's face after an event — show emotional impact      |

## Narrative Continuity Rules

1. **180° Rule:** Keep the camera on the same side of an action axis. Never cross the line between two characters — it reverses screen direction.
2. **Screen Direction:** If a character moves left-to-right in one shot, they must continue left-to-right in the next unless a neutral shot resets direction.
3. **Eyeline Match:** If a character looks at something off-screen, the next shot must show what they're looking at, from roughly their POV angle.
4. **Match on Action:** Cut mid-action (e.g., a hand reaching for a door handle) — the action continues in the new shot.
5. **Temporal Logic:** Shots must follow logical time sequence unless a flashback/flash-forward is clearly signalled (colour grade, title card, audio cue).

## Reversal and Twist Shots

A reversal is a moment where the story or viewer's understanding flips. Reinforce it visually:

- **Reveal shot:** Start tight (CU or MCU), then pull back or pan to reveal something the viewer didn't know was there.
- **Reframe:** A character who was dominant (low angle, large in frame) is suddenly small (high angle, EWS) — power has shifted.
- **Smash cut to silence:** After a loud, chaotic sequence, cut to a static wide shot in silence.
- **POV reversal:** Show what the character sees, then cut to the character's face — the reaction recontextualises what we just saw.

## Genre Visual Vocabulary

Different genres have established visual grammar. Know when to use it and when to break it:

| Genre           | Default Visual Language                                        | When to Break It                                   |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Drama           | Slow dolly ins, natural light, CU-heavy, J/L-cuts              | Use static wide at climax for emotional weight     |
| Action          | Handheld, quick cuts, wide-to-tight progression, canted angles | Slow-motion ECU at impact for contrast             |
| Horror/Thriller | Static long takes, negative space, shallow DOF, low angle      | Sudden cut to extreme brightness for reversal      |
| Romance         | Soft light, golden hour, dolly ins, warm colour grade          | One cold wide shot for separation/longing          |
| Comedy          | Eye level, neutral coverage, wide reactions, static camera     | Push in to ECU on punchline object for punctuation |
| Documentary     | Handheld observational, zoom ins, real environment light       | Static wide to signal significance/gravity         |
| Brand/Product   | Clean angles, hero lighting, smooth camera movement            | Unexpected POV or texture insert for memorability  |
| Music Video     | Beat-matched cuts, visual motifs, experimental angles          | Long uncut take for emotional contrast             |

## Pacing and Rhythm

Shot rhythm is the heartbeat of the piece. Control it:

- **Fast cutting (< 2s/shot):** Urgency, excitement, overwhelm, action
- **Medium rhythm (2–4s/shot):** Conversation, movement, engagement
- **Slow burn (4–8s/shot):** Tension, contemplation, emotional weight
- **Held shot (> 8s):** Revelation, confrontation, dread, or profound stillness

Never hold the same rhythm throughout. Build and release. Breathe.

## Prompt Writing for AI Generation

When writing IMAGE PROMPT and VIDEO PROMPT:

- Lead with the most visually dominant element
- State the shot type explicitly (e.g., "extreme wide shot", "close-up", "over-the-shoulder")
- State the camera movement for VIDEO PROMPT (e.g., "slow dolly in", "static", "handheld")
- Specify lighting quality and mood
- Add style reference (cinematic, photorealistic, film grain, anamorphic lens flare, etc.)
- Keep under 150 words
- Avoid abstract concepts — describe what the camera _literally_ sees
- For VIDEO PROMPT: describe the motion — what moves, in which direction, at what speed
- Match the prompt's emotional register to the narrative beat (e.g., a tense confrontation prompt should feel different from a tender reunion prompt, even at the same shot scale)
